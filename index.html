About Me

I am a Ph.D. Candidate in Applied Economics at the University of Florida (Advisor: Dr. Zhengfei Guan). I previously completed an M.S. in Applied and Computational Mathematics at Johns Hopkins University (Advisor: Prof. James Spall), a Certificate in International Studies at the Hopkins Nanjing Center, an M.Phil. in Economics at Wuhan University, and a B.S. in Economics at Tongji University.

My research focuses on the economics of innovation and technological change, the economics of AI, industrial organization and production economics, and environmental and resource economics. I combine theoretical models with causal inference, machine learning, and large-scale firm, regional, and agricultural data to study how new technologies affect productivity, market power, resource allocation, and environmental outcomes.

Research
Current Research

The Unequal Returns to AI Adoption: Productivity and Markups in the Chinese and U.S. Industrial Sectors

  • Examines the divergent effects of AI adoption on productivity and markups using listed-firm data from the Chinese and U.S. industrial sectors from 2015 to 2024.
  • Combines text-based measures of AI adoption, difference-in-differences estimation, a conceptual framework, and a Kalman-filtered Itô process to project future AI expansion.
  • Finds that AI primarily raises markups in China but improves productivity in the United States, reflecting differences in adoption costs and organizational integration.
Current Research

The Economic Value of Precision Agriculture under Drought Risk: Evidence from U.S. Corn Counties

  • Examines whether precision agriculture mitigates the effects of drought on corn yields and crop insurance losses using county-level data from major U.S. corn-producing states.
  • Combines county-year panel models, entropy balancing, staggered difference-in-differences, machine learning, and a dynamic stochastic welfare framework.
  • Finds that precision agriculture reduces drought-related production and insurance losses and generates estimated benefits of approximately $5–$20 per acre per year.
Dissertation

Three Essays on State Strategies for Technological Innovation, Energy Transition, and Food Security under Geopolitical Risk

  • Develops a theoretical framework to explain how U.S. technology restrictions may stimulate indigenous innovation and accelerate technological catch-up in China, with supplementary evidence from patent and academic publication data.
  • Examines how geopolitical risk and food security concerns reshape agricultural trade and land-use strategies in import-dependent countries, leading to agricultural expansion, deforestation, ecological pressures, and productivity losses.
  • Investigates how geopolitical tensions and energy security concerns encourage fossil-fuel-dependent countries to diversify their energy systems and increase the share of renewable energy in electricity generation.
